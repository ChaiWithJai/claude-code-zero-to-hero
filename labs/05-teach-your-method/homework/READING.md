## Name the work only you can direct

Write a small inventory of the work you do in a day or week. Label what draws on your judgment, what follows a repeatable method, and what drains attention. These labels are choices to examine, not permanent identities.

At the center of your gravity map, place the decisions you want to own. In the personal ring, place support that helps you think or act. In the professional ring, place support that depends on work context, collaboration, or account access. Something can move between rings as you learn.

Expertise becomes easier to delegate when you can show examples of a good result, explain trade-offs, and name exceptions. You do not need to prove a fixed genius or monetize the exercise. Begin with material you can appropriately use and a method you want to make clearer.

## Teach your sidekick one method

Choose a low-stakes method you already use. Describe what starts it, the inputs, the steps you usually take, the exceptions, and the visible result. Include a small example. An agent cannot reliably follow a preference you have not made explicit.

Write your instructions in the binder first. During this course, paste them into the task so you can see exactly what context you supplied. Storing persistent project instructions is an optional extension; instruction text itself is not an enforced permission boundary.

Run a short attempt with supplied or non-sensitive material. Review whether the result resembles your method, not merely whether it reads well. If a step is missing, improve the instruction and try another example. This is an instruction improvement loop; it does not mean you have retrained the underlying model.

## Use feedback to shape the support

Compare attempts side by side. Point to one improvement and one remaining limitation. Keep the evidence small: a corrected owner, a better category, or fewer unsupported claims is easier to evaluate than a vague claim that everything is smarter.

Correction effort matters. Record how much checking and fixing you did, and whether the output moved your own work forward. An enjoyable interaction can motivate practice, but it is a separate measure from reliable task performance.

Choose one next revision. Too many simultaneous changes make it hard to tell which instruction helped. When a result meets your criteria, save the brief and the checked output in your binder. When it does not, narrow the task or stop. A thoughtful stop is a valid decision.
